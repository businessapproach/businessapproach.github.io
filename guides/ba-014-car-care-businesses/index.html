Plenty of drivers would happily pay someone to handle the small jobs in their own driveway. Each idea below starts with one store-bought product we priced. The cheapest is glass treating, at about $7 for a 16 oz bottle of Rain-X, and the most expensive is tire top-ups, at about $38 for a cordless tire inflator.

These jobs are quick, so many people bundle a few into one visit. Work only where the owner says it's OK, follow each product's label, and let happy neighbors spread the word.

01

Tire Top-Ups

~$38 to start

You check each tire's pressure and fill it to the number on the sticker inside the driver's door, right in the customer's driveway. Cold weather lowers tire pressure, so many drivers need a top-up as soon as temperatures drop. It takes a few minutes per car, which makes it an easy add-on to a wash.

Good fit if: you like quick, simple jobs, don't mind working outside in the cold, and can stick to a routine for repeat customers.

Your starter kit

  • AstroAI cordless tire inflator, 160 PSI — ~$38 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

Charge the inflator fully before each round of visits, and double-check its reading against a simple tire gauge now and then.

What to charge

We couldn't find a reliable national cost guide for a tire top-up visit; it's usually a small add-on rather than a job on its own. Check what local mobile detailers and handymen charge for a short visit, and consider a small flat price per car or including it free in a wash bundle.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Post in your neighborhood Facebook group or Nextdoor on the first cold morning, when many dashboards light up with a low-tire warning.
  2. Offer free tire checks to older neighbors and busy parents you know, and ask for a review.
  3. Add it to every wash you do, so each customer sees the value and books you again.
Watch out: fill tires to the pressure on the door sticker or owner's manual, never to the maximum printed on the tire itself. If a tire keeps losing air, has a nail in it or looks damaged, tell the owner it needs a tire shop. Only work in driveways or parking spots, never next to traffic.

Headlight Restore

~$23 to start

You use a restoration kit to sand, polish and seal cloudy, yellowed plastic headlight covers so they look clearer. The before-and-after is easy to see, which makes this one of the easiest jobs to show off with photos.

Good fit if: you're patient, careful with your hands, and enjoy work with a dramatic before-and-after.

Your starter kit

  • Sylvania HRK.BX headlight restoration kit — ~$23 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

Read the kit's directions before your first job and practice on your own car or a friend's. Check how many headlights one kit covers so you know when to buy the next one.

What customers pay pros

Thumbtack puts the national average cost of mobile headlight restoration at $153-$267, with a low-end cost of $103 and a high-end cost of $392. HomeGuide lists headlight restoration as a detailing service at $50 – $125+. (Thumbtack)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Restore one headlight on your own car or a friend's and post the side-by-side photo in your neighborhood group.
  2. Offer a discounted first job to three people you know with older cars, in exchange for honest reviews.
  3. Ask local used-car sellers and people listing cars on Marketplace whether they'd like their headlights cleared up before a sale.
Watch out: tape off the paint around each headlight before sanding, wear gloves, and follow the kit's directions for the sealant. Restoring the plastic won't fix cracked covers, water inside the light or a burned-out bulb, so tell the owner those need a shop.
03

Hand Washes

~$23 to start

You hand-wash and dry the outside of a customer's car at their home, rinse the wheels and clean the windows. Regular customers often want a wash every few weeks in winter to get salt and grime off.

Good fit if: you don't mind physical work, take pride in a spotless finish, and like repeat customers.

Your starter kit

  • Chemical Guys Clean & Shine 4-item wash kit — ~$23 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

You'll also need water and a bucket. Most customers let you use their hose; agree on it before the visit.

What customers pay pros

HomeGuide lists an exterior-only basic wash & dry at $40 – $150. Its basic exterior detailing package (hand wash and dry, clean the windows, clean the tires) costs $50 – $100. (HomeGuide)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Wash your own family's cars and a neighbor's, and post the results (with permission) in your neighborhood group with a clear flat price.
  2. Offer a discount for booking several cars on the same street on the same day.
  3. Leave a simple card with each customer offering a regular wash every few weeks.
Watch out: some cities and HOAs limit washing cars where soapy water runs into storm drains, so check local rules and the customer's HOA. Don't wash when it's below freezing, since water can freeze on the car, locks and driveway.
04

Glass Treating

~$7 to start

You clean the outside glass and apply a rain-repellent treatment so water beads up and rolls off the windshield and windows. It's a quick, cheap add-on that customers notice the next time it rains.

Good fit if: you're careful and tidy, and like small jobs that make a big difference.

Your starter kit

  • Rain-X Original glass treatment, 16 oz — ~$7 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

One bottle covers several cars. Clean microfiber towels are all you need besides the treatment.

What to charge

We couldn't find a reliable national cost guide for a glass-treatment visit; detailers usually sell it as a small add-on to a wash. Check what local detailers charge for the add-on, and consider a small flat price per car, or include it in a winter wash bundle.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Offer it as an add-on to every hand wash at a small extra price.
  2. Treat your own windshield and share a short video of rain beading off it in your neighborhood group.
  3. Mention it to people who drive a lot at night or on the highway, like commuters and delivery drivers.
Watch out: apply it only to the outside of clean, dry glass, following the label, and keep it off paint and the inside of windows. Tell customers that new treatments can make old wiper blades chatter, and that worn blades may need replacing.
05

Spray Waxing

~$13 to start

After a wash, you spray on a wax and buff it off with microfiber towels for extra shine and a protective layer. It's a natural upgrade to sell to your wash customers.

Good fit if: you like a glossy, finished look and don't mind a bit of arm work.

Your starter kit

  • Turtle Wax ICE spray wax, 20 oz — ~$13 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

Always wash and dry the car first; waxing over dirt can scratch the paint. Keep a separate set of clean towels just for buffing.

What customers pay pros

HomeGuide lists an exterior-only wash & wax at $100 – $250. (HomeGuide)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Offer every wash customer a wax upgrade at a clear extra price.
  2. Wax one panel of a friend's car to show the difference, then offer to do the rest.
  3. Post before-and-after photos (with permission) in your neighborhood group.
Watch out: follow the label for where and when to apply it; most waxes work best on a cool surface out of direct sun. Don't promise it will fix scratches, faded paint or rust; that's work for a body shop or a professional detailer.
06

Odor Removal

~$8 to start

You clean out trash, vacuum and wipe down the inside of a car, then treat it with an odor-eliminating mist to leave it smelling fresh. It pairs well with an interior tidy-up or a wash.

Good fit if: you don't mind messy interiors and like leaving things clean and fresh.

Your starter kit

  • Meguiar's Whole Car Air Re-Fresher G16402 — ~$8 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

A vacuum and a few clean cloths make the treatment work much better, since removing the source of the smell comes first. Many customers will let you use their own vacuum.

What to charge

We couldn't find a reliable national cost guide for a quick odor treatment on its own. HomeGuide lists severe odor removal as a detailing add-on without a separate price, and puts interior-only detailing at $100 – $250 for a much fuller job. Check what local detailers charge, and price a simple freshen-up visit below a full interior detail. See HomeGuide's car detailing cost guide.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Offer it to parents, pet owners and rideshare drivers you know; they're often the people most bothered by car smells.
  2. Bundle it with a hand wash as an inside-and-out visit at one flat price.
  3. Ask happy customers to mention you in local parent and pet-owner groups.
Watch out: follow the product's label, keep doors open for airflow, and keep kids and pets out of the car while it airs out. Strong smoke, mold or spilled-milk smells may need a professional detailer, so be honest with customers about what a quick treatment can and can't fix.

Before you take your first job

  1. Check local rules. Search "[your city] business license" to see whether you need one for a mobile car-care business. Rules vary by city and state, and some places and HOAs limit washing cars where runoff goes into storm drains.
  2. Get permission. Only work in a customer's own driveway or a spot where the property owner has said it's OK.
  3. Put it in writing. Send a short message with the jobs, date, price and whose water and power you'll use before every visit.
  4. Bundle your visits. Offer a simple winter bundle, like a wash, glass treatment and tire top-up in one visit at one flat price.
  5. Track every dollar. Side income is taxable, so record what you earn and spend, keep supply receipts, and set some aside.

FAQ

Which of these is cheapest to start?

Glass treating, at about $7 for a 16 oz bottle of Rain-X. Odor removal starts at about $8 for a Meguiar's whole-car odor eliminator.

Do I need a business license?

It depends on your city and state. Many places require a general business license for paid services, even part-time, and some have rules about mobile car washing and water runoff. Check your city's website or call the clerk's office before you start.

How much can I make?

We don't promise earnings. Where we show prices, they're what customers typically pay professionals, from published cost guides. As a beginner, expect to charge less until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Can I wash cars in winter?

Yes, on milder days. Avoid washing when it's below freezing, since water can freeze on the car, door locks and driveway. Glass treating, tire top-ups and odor removal work any time.

Can teens do these?

Hand washes, glass treating and odor removal can work for teens with a parent's OK, working for people they know. Check your state's rules for working minors.
