01

Assembly Help

~$69 to start

You assemble flat-pack furniture people bought online or in store: bookshelves, desks, dressers, bed frames, patio sets and kids' furniture. You follow the instructions, anchor tall pieces to the wall when the kit includes a tip-over strap, and take the cardboard away if the client wants.

Good fit if: you're patient with instructions, like seeing a project come together, and can lift and move boxes safely.

Your starter kit

  • RYOBI ONE+ P1811 compact drill kit with 2 batteries — ~$69 (Home Depot · See options on Amazon)

Set the drill's clutch low and finish screws by hand; overtightening strips the particleboard most flat-pack furniture is made of.

What customers pay pros

Fixr says furniture assembly costs range from $150 to $200, with a national average of $175, and assemblers who charge by the hour run $50 to $100, depending on the number of items. (Fixr)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Tell friends and neighbors you assemble furniture, and offer your first few jobs at a discount in exchange for a review.
  2. Post a clear photo of a finished piece in your neighborhood Facebook group or Nextdoor with a simple flat price per item.
  3. Ask every happy client if they have another piece waiting; people often buy several at once when they move.
Watch out: check every part against the instruction sheet before you start, and lift heavy boxes with a second person. Always install the anti-tip strap on dressers and tall bookshelves if the kit includes one; tip-overs can seriously hurt kids.
02

TV Mounting

~$18 to start

You mount TVs on drywall using the client's wall bracket, find the studs, level the bracket, and hang the TV so it's secure and straight. Many clients also want the cables tidied with a cord cover.

Good fit if: you're careful, measure twice, and are comfortable drilling into walls.

Your starter kit

  • Zircon StudSensor HD55 stud finder — ~$18 (Home Depot · See options on Amazon)
  • A drill and a level (see Assembly Help and Shelf Hanging) — price varies (hardware stores)

The post's ~$18 covers the stud finder only. The client usually buys the wall bracket for their TV size.

What customers pay pros

Fixr says TV mount costs range from $100 to $300, with a national average of $200, and the average TV wall mount installation service costs from $75 to $200. (Fixr)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Offer to mount a TV for a friend or family member, and take before-and-after photos (with permission).
  2. Post in local groups around holidays and big game days, when people buy new TVs.
  3. Keep your price simple, like one flat price per TV up to a set screen size, plus an add-on for cord covers.
Watch out: always mount into studs, or use the bracket maker's approved anchors, and check the bracket's weight rating. Use two people to lift the TV. Don't mount over fireplaces, on brick or plaster, or move outlets inside walls unless you're qualified; electrical work often needs a licensed electrician.
03

Re-Caulking

~$17 to start

You scrape out old, cracked or moldy caulk around tubs, showers and sinks, clean and dry the joint, and lay a fresh, smooth bead of silicone. It's a quick job that makes a bathroom look much cleaner.

Good fit if: you have a steady hand, don't mind cleaning, and take pride in neat finishing work.

Your starter kit

  • Anvil caulk gun — ~$6 (Home Depot · See options on Amazon)
  • GE Silicone 2 kitchen and bath sealant, 10.1 oz — ~$11 (Home Depot · See options on Amazon)

The post's ~$17 is the caulk gun ($5.98) plus one tube of silicone ($10.98). Painter's tape on both sides of the joint makes clean lines much easier.

What customers pay pros

Fixr says you can caulk a bathtub yourself for around $20 in supplies, or hire a pro to do the work for between $50 and $100. (Fixr)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Re-caulk your own tub first and use the before-and-after photos as your sample.
  2. Reach out to landlords and property managers; they need tubs re-caulked between tenants.
  3. Offer a bundle, like tub plus bathroom sink, for one flat price.
Watch out: open a window or run the fan, wear gloves, and read the label on any caulk or mold cleaner you use. Tell clients how long to wait before using the shower so the caulk can cure. If the wall behind the tub feels soft or water is leaking, that's a job for a plumber or contractor.
04

Draft Proofing

~$10 to start

You find and seal the gaps that let cold air in around doors: installing door sweeps, replacing worn weatherstripping and sealing small gaps. It's a popular job right before winter.

Good fit if: you notice small details, like simple practical jobs, and want a service with clear seasonal demand.

Your starter kit

  • Frost King 36 in aluminum door sweep — ~$10 (Home Depot · See options on Amazon)
  • Weatherstripping for each job — price varies (hardware stores)

Measure the door's width before you buy; sweeps come in standard sizes and some need cutting to fit.

What customers pay pros

Fixr says the cost to weatherstrip a door is between $35 and $90 per door, and the cost of a door sweep is between $13 and $20 per linear foot. (Fixr)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Offer a fall or winter special for older neighbors, like one flat price per door including the sweep.
  2. Post a short video of daylight showing under a door before and after a sweep; it explains the job instantly.
  3. Ask each client if other doors or windows feel drafty while you're there.
Watch out: don't block a door so tightly that it sticks or won't close; test it several times. Leave furnace, gas appliance and window replacement work to licensed pros.
05

Shelf Hanging

~$30 to start

You hang shelves, mirrors, picture ledges and wall organizers so they're level, secure and in the right spot. Many clients book several small hanging jobs in one visit.

Good fit if: you have a good eye for straight lines and spacing, and you're comfortable using a drill and anchors.

Your starter kit

  • Empire 24 in box level — ~$30 (Home Depot · See options on Amazon)
  • Wall anchors and screws for each job — price varies (hardware stores)

Match the anchor to the wall and the weight. Heavy shelves should go into studs.

What customers pay pros

Taskrabbit's cost guide lists mounting and installation at $50–$85 per hour, and an estimated $90–$180 for mirrors/shelves. (Taskrabbit)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Hang shelves for a friend who just moved, and ask for a review and permission to share photos.
  2. Offer a "hang everything" visit for new movers: shelves, mirrors and pictures for one flat price up to a set number of items.
  3. Leave a simple card with every client so they can pass your number on.
Watch out: use a stud finder before drilling to avoid pipes and wires, and never drill near outlets or switches. Don't promise a shelf will hold more than its brackets and anchors are rated for.
06

Small Fixes

~$5 to start

You handle the little jobs on everyone's list: tightening loose hinges and handles, adjusting cabinet doors, replacing cabinet knobs, fixing a sticking drawer and swapping out a toilet seat. Clients often save up several small jobs for one visit.

Good fit if: you like solving small problems, are friendly with clients, and want the cheapest possible start.

Your starter kit

  • Husky 4-in-1 pocket screwdriver — ~$5 (Home Depot · See options on Amazon)

Start with the screwdriver and buy other tools only when a paid job needs them.

What customers pay pros

Fixr says the hourly rate for a handyman ranges from $50 to $100, with most charging around $65 an hour. It also notes that many handymen set a minimum charge for small jobs, and the most common is $130. (Fixr)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Ask older neighbors and busy parents for their "little jobs" list and offer to tackle it in one visit.
  2. Post a simple menu of small jobs with flat prices in your neighborhood group.
  3. Follow up a month later with past clients; most homes have a new small job by then.
Watch out: stay within small, simple repairs. Many states require a contractor or handyman license above a certain job size, and electrical, plumbing and gas work usually needs a licensed pro, so check your state's rules before you take bigger jobs.

Before you take your first job

  1. Check local rules. Search "[your city] business license" and "[your state] handyman license". Rules vary by city and state, and some states limit what an unlicensed handyman can do.
  2. Pick one or two services. Start with the jobs you can already do well, and add more once you have reviews.
  3. Put it in writing. Send a short written quote with the job, flat price and what's not included before every visit.
  4. Think about insurance. You're working in people's homes, so look into basic liability insurance before you take paid jobs.
  5. Track every dollar. Side income is taxable, so keep receipts for tools and supplies, record what you earn, and set some aside.
Pro tip

List flat prices per job instead of hourly rates; clients say yes faster.

FAQ

Which of these is cheapest to start?

Small fixes, at about $5 for a 4-in-1 pocket screwdriver. Draft proofing starts at about $10 for a door sweep, and re-caulking at about $17 for a caulk gun and one tube of silicone.

Do I need a license?

It depends on your city and state. Many places require a general business license, and some states require a contractor or handyman license for jobs above a certain size. Electrical, plumbing and gas work usually needs a licensed pro. Check your city and state websites before you start.

How much can I make?

We don't promise earnings. The prices on this page are what customers typically pay professionals, from published cost guides. As a beginner, expect to charge less until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Should I charge by the hour or by the job?

Many beginners find flat prices per job easier to sell, because clients know the cost up front. Time yourself on your first few jobs so you know how long each one takes, and set prices that cover your time and supplies.

Can teens do these?

Assembly help and small fixes can work for older teens with a parent's OK, ideally working with an adult. Leave TV mounting and drilling into walls to adults. Always have a parent know where you're working, and check your state's rules for working minors.
