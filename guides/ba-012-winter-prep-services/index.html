Every fall, homeowners have the same short list of jobs to do before the first freeze: cover the outdoor faucets, seal drafty windows and doors, wrap exposed pipes, change the furnace filter and check the smoke alarms. None of it is hard, but plenty of people don't have the time, the ladder or the confidence to do it. Each idea below starts with one inexpensive item from a real store page. The cheapest is pipe wrapping, at about $3 for a 6 ft foam sleeve, and the most expensive is alarm checks, at about $16 for a 4-pack of 9V batteries.

Below are the six ideas from our post, with the exact product we priced, what customers pay professionals where a reliable price guide exists, and the simplest way to land your first three clients. These are small, quick jobs, so many people bundle several of them into one visit. Keep it simple, stay off anything that needs a licensed plumber, electrician or HVAC tech, and let happy neighbors spread the word.

01

Faucet Covers

~$6 to start

You disconnect garden hoses, drain them, and fit an insulated cover over each outdoor faucet so it's less likely to freeze and crack. It takes a few minutes per faucet, which makes it an easy add-on to any other winter-prep visit.

Good fit if: you don't mind working outside in the cold and like quick, simple jobs you can do in a row on the same street.

Your starter kit

  • MERILER insulated outdoor faucet cover — ~$6 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

Most clients will need one cover per outdoor faucet, so ask how many they have and agree whether you bring the covers or they buy them.

What to charge

We couldn't find a reliable national cost guide for faucet-cover visits; it's usually a small add-on rather than a job on its own. Check what local handymen and neighborhood listings charge for a short visit, and consider a simple flat price per faucet, with covers billed at what you paid.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Post in your neighborhood Facebook group or Nextdoor when the first frost is in the forecast, with a flat price per faucet.
  2. Offer it as a free add-on to your first few clients for other winter-prep jobs, and ask for a review.
  3. Ask happy clients to tell next-door neighbors; you can do several houses on one street in an hour.
Watch out: only cover the faucet and disconnect hoses. Don't shut off or open indoor valves or touch the plumbing unless the homeowner does it or asks you to and shows you how. If a faucet is already leaking or cracked, tell the owner it needs a plumber.
02

Window Film

~$6 to start

You put up clear indoor shrink film over drafty windows. The film sticks to the window trim with double-sided tape and is tightened with a hair dryer, leaving a clear layer that cuts drafts. Many clients want it done once in the fall and taken down in spring.

Good fit if: you're patient, careful with measuring and tape, and like tidy, finished-looking work.

Your starter kit

  • Frost King V73H indoor shrink window kit — ~$6 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

Check the kit's size and how many windows it covers against the client's windows before you buy. A hair dryer is the only other tool you need; most clients have one you can use.

What to charge

We couldn't find a reliable national cost guide for putting up shrink-film window kits; most pro window-film prices are for tinting or security film, which is a different job. Check local handyman listings for a short visit, and consider pricing per window, with the kits billed at what you paid.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Ask older neighbors and renters you know whether any windows feel drafty, and offer to seal one window free so they can see the result.
  2. Post before-and-after photos (with permission, no addresses) in your neighborhood group.
  3. Offer a spring take-down visit at the same time, so you get booked twice.
Watch out: tape can pull paint or finish off some window trim when it's removed, so tell the client first and test a small spot if they're worried. Keep the hair dryer moving, and don't block windows that are needed as emergency exits, especially in bedrooms. Renters should check with their landlord first.
03

Pipe Wrapping

~$3 to start

You fit foam insulation sleeves over exposed water pipes in garages, basements and crawl spaces, so they lose less heat and are less likely to freeze. The foam is pre-slit, so it snaps over the pipe and the seam is closed with its own adhesive strip or tape.

Good fit if: you don't mind tight, dusty spaces and like quick jobs with a clear before-and-after.

Your starter kit

  • Everbilt 1/2 in x 6 ft foam pipe insulation — ~$3 (Home Depot · See options on Amazon)

Measure the pipe size and how many feet are exposed before you buy; foam sleeves are sized to the pipe. A tape measure and scissors or a utility knife are all you need to cut it to length.

What to charge

We couldn't find a current national cost guide for small pipe-wrapping visits. For context, a Fixr winterizing guide (published 2021) lists insulating hot water pipes at $100 - $1,000 for a full project, which is a much bigger job than wrapping a few exposed pipes. Check local handyman listings and price a short visit in that local range, with foam billed at what you paid. See Fixr's winterizing guide.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Offer free pipe checks to neighbors with unheated garages or basements, and quote on the spot.
  2. Bundle it with faucet covers as a simple "freeze prep" visit at one flat price.
  3. Ask local landlords who rent out older homes whether they'd like their exposed pipes wrapped before winter.
Watch out: never cut into, move or unscrew any pipe; if you see leaks, corrosion or damage, tell the owner to call a plumber. Wear gloves and a dust mask in crawl spaces and attics, watch for nails and wiring, and don't go into a crawl space alone. Keep foam away from furnace and water-heater flues.

Filter Swaps

~$12 to start

You replace the disposable air filter on a client's furnace with a new one of the right size, and write the date on it so they know when it's due again. Many clients like a reminder visit every few months.

Good fit if: you're reliable with schedules and like simple repeat visits for the same clients.

Your starter kit

  • Filtrete 16x25x1 MPR 800 furnace filter — ~$12 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

Filters come in many sizes. Check the size printed on the client's current filter before you buy, and use the same size and type unless the owner says otherwise.

What to charge

We couldn't find a reliable national cost guide for a filter-swap visit on its own; HVAC companies usually include it in a full furnace tune-up, which is a different, licensed job. Check what local handymen charge for a short visit, and consider a simple flat price per visit, with the filter billed at what you paid. A repeat schedule (for example, every few months) gives clients a reason to book you again.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Offer filter swaps to older neighbors and busy families you know, with a reminder text when the next one is due.
  2. Bundle a filter swap with an alarm check as a simple "home safety visit".
  3. Ask happy clients for one referral each, and keep a list of filter sizes so repeat visits are quick.
Watch out: turn the furnace or thermostat off before you open the filter slot, and put the filter in with the airflow arrow pointing the right way. Only change the filter; don't open burner panels, relight pilots or touch gas lines, which is work for a licensed HVAC tech. If you smell gas, leave and tell the owner to call the gas company.
05

Alarm Checks

~$16 to start

You test each smoke alarm with its test button, swap in fresh batteries where needed, and give the homeowner a short list of any alarms that didn't work or look old. It's a quick, valuable visit, especially for older neighbors who shouldn't be on a ladder.

Good fit if: you're careful, steady on a step ladder, and good at explaining things simply.

Your starter kit

  • Energizer Max 9V batteries, 4-count — ~$16 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

Not every alarm uses 9V batteries; some take AA batteries and some have sealed batteries that can't be changed. Check each alarm's label before you buy.

What to charge

We couldn't find a national cost guide for a battery-and-test visit. For context, Fixr says installing a smoke detector costs a national average of $70 to $150, and that labor makes up about $40 of that per detector; a battery swap is quicker than installing a new alarm. Check local handyman listings for a short visit, and bill batteries at what you paid. See Fixr's smoke detector cost guide.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Offer alarm checks to older neighbors and their adult children, who often look for help like this for their parents.
  2. Time a post in your neighborhood group around daylight saving time changes, when many people are reminded to check their alarms.
  3. Bundle it with filter swaps, and give clients a written note of the date and what you checked.
Watch out: use a sturdy step ladder, keep three points of contact, and don't climb if you're alone in the house. Only swap batteries and test alarms; leave any wiring and hardwired alarm replacement to a licensed electrician. If an alarm doesn't work after a new battery or is past the date printed on it, tell the owner it needs replacing.

Weatherstripping

~$10 to start

You find the drafty gaps around doors and windows, clean the surface, and apply self-adhesive rubber weather-strip tape so doors and windows close tighter. It's one of the most noticeable fixes a homeowner can feel the same day.

Good fit if: you're handy with simple tools, notice details, and like work where the result is easy to see and feel.

Your starter kit

  • Frost King V27WA rubber weather-strip tape, 10 ft — ~$10 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

Measure each door or window before you buy; one 10 ft roll may not cover a full door frame. Clean and dry the surface first so the adhesive holds.

What customers pay pros

Fixr says the cost to weatherstrip a door is between $35 and $90 per door. For a whole home, weatherstripping costs range from $200 to $600, with a national average of $350. (Fixr)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Offer a free draft check for a few neighbors, and quote per door on the spot.
  2. Weatherstrip your own door or a friend's, and share a short before-and-after video (with permission) in your neighborhood group.
  3. Ask local landlords and property managers whether they'd like doors in their rentals sealed before winter.
Watch out: don't seal a door or window so tightly it won't close or lock properly, and test each one with the client before you leave. Never block vents, and leave old, rotting frames or broken windows to a pro.

Before you take your first job

  1. Check local rules. Search "[your city] business license" to see whether you need one for a home-service business. Rules vary by city and state, and some places limit what unlicensed handymen can do.
  2. Stay in your lane. Stick to the simple jobs on this list. Anything involving gas, wiring or cutting into plumbing is work for a licensed pro.
  3. Put it in writing. Send a short message with the jobs, date, price and how you'll be paid back for supplies before every visit.
  4. Bundle your visits. Offer a simple "winter-prep visit" that covers several of these jobs at one flat price, so each trip is worth your time.
  5. Track every dollar. Side income is taxable, so record what you earn and spend, keep supply receipts, and set some aside.
Pro tip

Knock on doors after the first frost warning. That's when people say yes.

FAQ

Which of these is cheapest to start?

Pipe wrapping, at about $3 for a 6 ft foam sleeve. Faucet covers and window film each start at about $6.

Do I need a business license?

It depends on your city and state. Many places require a general business license for paid home services, even part-time, and some limit what handyman work you can do without a contractor's license. Check your city's website or call the clerk's office before you start.

How much can I make?

We don't promise earnings. Where we show prices, they're what customers typically pay professionals, from published cost guides. As a beginner, expect to charge less until you have reviews.

Should I buy the supplies or should the client?

Either works. Many beginners have the client buy the right-size filters, covers or film, or they buy them and bill the client exactly what they paid, with the receipt. Agree on it in writing before the visit.

Can teens do these?

Faucet covers, window film and weatherstripping can work for teens with a parent's OK, working for people they know. Anything on a ladder or in a crawl space is better with an adult nearby. Check your state's rules for working minors.
