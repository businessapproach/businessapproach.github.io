Light Hanging

~$150 to start

You hang the customer's outdoor holiday lights along gutters, rooflines, windows and bushes, then come back in January to take them down. Most of the job is planning the layout, safe ladder work and neat clipping.

Good fit if: you're comfortable on a ladder, like tidy detail work and can work a few evenings and weekends in November.

Your starter kit

  • Werner 6 ft fiberglass step ladder — ~$129 (Home Depot · See options on Amazon)
  • All-purpose gutter & shingle light clips — price varies by pack (any hardware store · See options on Amazon)

The post's ~$150 total is the ladder plus a pack of clips. Customers usually supply their own lights; if you supply them, charge for them separately.

What customers pay pros

Professional holiday light installation averages $240–$650 installed, or about $2.50–$5.00 per foot including lights. (HomeGuide)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Hang your own (or a friend's) lights first and take clear before/after photos at dusk.
  2. Post those photos in your neighborhood Facebook group or on Nextdoor with a simple offer: install now, take-down in January included.
  3. Knock on doors of one-story homes on your street, especially older neighbors who shouldn't be on ladders.
Watch out: stick to one-story rooflines until you're experienced, always have someone hold the ladder on uneven ground, and never staple or nail through gutters or shingles. Only plug lights into outdoor-rated outlets; leave any electrical wiring work to licensed electricians.

Snow Clearing

~$60 to start

You shovel driveways, walkways and steps after snowfall and spread ice melt on slippery spots. Customers value reliability most: being there before they need to leave for work.

Good fit if: you live where it snows, you're fine with early mornings and physical work, and your customers are within walking distance.

Your starter kit

  • Snow Joe 25 lb ice melt — ~$35 (Home Depot · See options on Amazon)
  • Combo snow shovel / pusher — price varies (any hardware store · See options on Amazon)

The post's ~$60 total is the ice melt plus a basic combo shovel.

What customers pay pros

Shoveling an average driveway typically costs $30–$70 per visit; seasonal contracts average $200–$600 per season. (HomeGuide)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Make a "storm list" before the first snow: ask neighbors if they want you to come automatically after every snowfall.
  2. Offer a simple per-visit price and a discounted season price, paid up front.
  3. Start with seniors and busy parents on your street; one reliable season usually means they book you again next year.
Watch out: shoveling heavy snow is hard on your back and heart. Lift small loads, push instead of lifting where you can and take breaks. Ask before using ice melt near plants, pets or new concrete.
03

Window Washing

~$50 to start

You clean glass, frames and sills, inside and out, so homes look their best before guests arrive. A good squeegee technique is most of the skill and takes an afternoon of practice.

Good fit if: you like quick, visible results and want something you can do year-round, not just in December.

Your starter kit

  • Unger Total Pro window cleaning kit (squeegee, scrubber, pole) — ~$46 (Home Depot · See options on Amazon)

What customers pay pros

Residential window cleaning averages $220 per visit, with a typical range of $150–$302, or about $4–$8 per window. (Angi)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Practice on your own windows until there are no streaks, then photograph a before/after pane in good light.
  2. Offer a "party-ready" package for the weeks before Thanksgiving and Christmas.
  3. Leave a short flyer with a per-window price at homes with lots of front-facing glass.
Watch out: start with ground-floor and interior windows only; second-story work on ladders is where injuries happen. On freezing days, stick to interior glass.

Gift Wrapping

~$30 to start

You wrap gifts beautifully for busy people and small shops. You can work from home with drop-off, go to customers' homes, or set up a table at a local shop or holiday market.

Good fit if: you're neat, patient and creative, and want a low-cost idea you can do indoors. This is also one of the easiest ideas for teens.

Your starter kit

  • Wrapping paper 3-pack — ~$13 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)
  • Clear tape and ribbon — price varies (any store · See options on Amazon)

The post's ~$30 total is paper plus tape and ribbon to start. Buy more as orders come in.

What customers pay pros

On Taskrabbit, gift wrapping services average about $20 per hour, varying by location and job size. (Taskrabbit)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Wrap 5 gifts with different styles and photograph them as your menu.
  2. Ask a local boutique if you can wrap at a table on busy weekends (they get happy customers, you get the jobs).
  3. Offer office drop-off: collect gifts at a workplace on Monday, return them wrapped on Wednesday.
Watch out: agree on paper and price before you start, and charge more for oversized or oddly shaped items. Keep a simple list of whose gifts are whose so nothing gets mixed up.

Leaf Cleanup

~$180 to start

You blow, rake and bag leaves from lawns, beds and driveways, then leave them where your town collects yard waste. It's peak season right now in most of the country.

Good fit if: you like outdoor work, have weekends free and live in an area with lots of trees.

Your starter kit

  • Ryobi 40V cordless leaf blower kit (battery + charger) — ~$179 (Home Depot · See options on Amazon)

What customers pay pros

Leaf removal averages $376, with most homeowners paying $190–$591; hourly rates run about $15–$60 depending on raking, blowing or vacuuming. (Angi)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Walk your neighborhood the weekend after a windy day and note the yards that are buried.
  2. Leave a simple door hanger: one-time cleanup price plus an optional second visit in December.
  3. Ask your first customers' neighbors; matching yards on one street saves you travel time.
Watch out: check your town's yard-waste rules (bags vs curb piles, pickup dates) and any leaf-blower hour limits before you start. Wear eye and hearing protection.
06

Car Interiors

~$150 to start

You vacuum, wipe down and refresh car interiors: seats, mats, cup holders, door panels and windows. Holiday travel and visiting family make December a busy month.

Good fit if: you're detail-oriented and can work at customers' driveways or have space at home.

Your starter kit

  • RIDGID 5 gallon wet/dry shop vac — ~$119 (Home Depot · See options on Amazon)
  • Microfiber cloths and interior cleaner spray — price varies (any auto store · See options on Amazon)

The post's ~$150 total is the shop vac plus cloths and an interior spray.

What customers pay pros

Car interior detailing typically costs $150–$350; a basic interior clean is about $150–$200. (HomeGuide)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Detail your own or a friend's car and take before/after photos of the worst spots (floor mats and cup holders sell it).
  2. Offer a "road trip ready" interior package in the two weeks before Thanksgiving and Christmas.
  3. Ask parents at school pickup or sports practice; family cars need it most.
Watch out: test any cleaner on a hidden spot first and don't promise to remove every stain. Ask before plugging into a customer's outlet, and remove valuables with the owner present.

Before you take your first job

  1. Check local rules. Search "[your city] business license" to see whether you need one. Some cities require a general business license even for small side work.
  2. Think about insurance. Ask a local agent about general liability insurance before ladder or yard work; a single accident can cost more than a season's income.
  3. Set clear prices. Write a simple price list (per visit, per window, per gift) and give written quotes by text so there are no surprises.
  4. Take payment simply. Use a payment app you already have and send a receipt for every job.
  5. Track every dollar. Keep receipts for tools and supplies; side income is taxable, so set some aside.
Pro tip

Ask every happy client for one neighbor's name. That's your next job.

FAQ

Which of these is cheapest to start?

Gift wrapping, at about $30 for paper, tape and ribbon. Window washing is next at about $50.

Do I need a business license?

It depends on your city and state. Many places require a general business license for paid services, even part-time. Search your city's website or call the clerk's office before you start.

How much can I make?

We don't promise earnings. The ranges above are what customers typically pay professionals, from published cost guides. As a beginner, expect to charge less until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Can teens do these?

Gift wrapping, window washing (ground floor) and leaf cleanup are good starter jobs for teens with a parent's OK. Skip ladder work and check your state's rules for working minors.

What if it doesn't snow where I live?

Swap snow clearing for leaf cleanup or window washing. Both work in milder climates and keep going after the holidays.
