Busy weeks, and especially the holidays, leave a lot of people short on time for the small jobs: mailing packages, picking up groceries, returning things, standing in line. Each idea below starts with one inexpensive item and the car or transit pass you already use. The most expensive one is package pickup, at about $40 for a folding cart that holds 90 lb. The cheapest, gift shopping, is free to start with a free Google Voice number.

Below are the six ideas from our post, with the exact product we priced on a real store page, what customers typically pay for the same errand where a reliable price guide exists, and the simplest way to land your first three clients. Start with people you know, be on time, keep every receipt, and let happy clients send their friends your way.

01

Package Pickup

~$40 to start

You collect packages from clients' homes or offices and take them to the post office or a shipping store, or pick up deliveries held at a pickup point. It's especially handy in November and December, when people are mailing gifts and lines are long.

Good fit if: you have a reliable car or live close to a post office, are organized, and are happy to lift and carry boxes.

Your starter kit

  • DMI compact folding shopping cart (holds 90 lb) — ~$40 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

Take a photo of each package and keep the shipping receipt for the client. Lift with your legs, and split heavy loads into more than one trip.

What customers pay pros

Taskrabbit's errand cost guide puts mailing packages at $20 to $45 per errand. Errand running overall averages $28 per hour in the U.S., and most states average between $25.00 and $35.50 per hour. (Taskrabbit)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Post in your neighborhood Facebook group or Nextdoor in early November offering holiday package drop-offs, with a simple flat price per trip.
  2. Ask small home businesses you know (Etsy sellers, bakers, crafters) if they'd like a regular weekly post office run.
  3. Leave a short flyer with older neighbors or at a senior center, if allowed, and ask happy clients to pass your number along.
Watch out: never accept a package if you don't know what's inside, and don't ship anything the carrier doesn't allow. Agree in writing who pays postage and how, and hand over every receipt and tracking number the same day.
02

Grocery Runs

~$15 to start

You shop from a client's list, or pick up an order they've already placed, and bring it to their door. Regular weekly clients are the goal: same day, same store, same routine.

Good fit if: you're careful with lists, notice details like ripe produce and expiration dates, and like a steady routine.

Your starter kit

  • Tripumer insulated grocery bags, 3-pack — ~$15 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

Shop cold and frozen items last, keep them in the insulated bags, and go straight to the client's home after checkout.

What customers pay pros

Taskrabbit's errand cost guide puts grocery pickup at $20 to $50 and grocery shopping at $30 to $60 per errand. The cost of the groceries themselves is added on top. (Taskrabbit)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Offer one free or discounted grocery run to a family member or neighbor in exchange for an honest review.
  2. Ask at your place of worship, a local parents' group or a senior community whether anyone needs a regular weekly shopper.
  3. Offer a set weekly time slot so clients can count on you, and ask each happy client for one referral.
Watch out: agree in advance how you'll be paid back for groceries, and give the client the receipt every time. Keep a short substitution plan for out-of-stock items, and keep cold food cold. Don't buy alcohol or medicine for a client unless you know your state's rules allow it.
03

Gift Shopping

Free to start

You shop for gifts for clients who don't have the time: holiday lists, birthdays, teacher and coworker gifts, or thank-you gifts for a small business. Many clients also like you to wrap and deliver them.

Good fit if: you enjoy shopping, are good at finding the right thing on a budget, and stay organized with lots of names and sizes.

Your starter kit

  • Google Voice number for client calls and texts — Free (Google)

A separate number keeps your personal phone private. Google says its no-charge plan is for personal, consumer use and is only available in the US; it needs a personal Gmail account and a phone number to forward calls to.

What customers pay pros

There's no separate national price guide for gift-shopping errands, so most errand runners charge their usual hourly rate. Taskrabbit's errand cost guide puts errand running at $28 per hour on average in the U.S., with the cost of the items bought added to the bill. (Taskrabbit)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Offer a holiday gift-shopping package to coworkers or friends with long lists, with a clear hourly price and an agreed budget per gift.
  2. Ask local small business owners if they'd like help buying and delivering client or employee thank-you gifts.
  3. Share photos of nicely wrapped gifts (with permission, no names) in your neighborhood group to show your work.
Watch out: agree on the budget for each gift in writing before you shop, and never go over it without asking. Keep every receipt and gift receipt, and don't use your own credit card for large purchases unless you're sure you'll be paid back right away.
04

Return Runs

~$13 to start

You take clients' unwanted purchases back to the store, or pack up online returns and drop them at a shipping location. It's busiest right after the holidays and after big sales.

Good fit if: you're patient in customer service lines and careful about paperwork and receipts.

Your starter kit

  • Scotch heavy duty packaging tape with dispenser — ~$13 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

Photograph each item and its return label before you seal the box, and keep the drop-off receipt for the client.

What customers pay pros

Taskrabbit's errand cost guide puts store returns at $20 to $40 per errand. (Taskrabbit)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Post a simple "holiday returns" offer in your neighborhood group for the last week of December and early January.
  2. Offer to bundle returns for several neighbors on the same street into one trip, at a lower price each.
  3. Ask happy clients to keep your number for the next big sale season.
Watch out: store refunds usually go back to the original card, so the client stays in control of the money. Check each store's return window and rules before you go, and only return items with the client's receipt or order number.
05

Line Sitting

~$11 to start

You wait in line so your client doesn't have to: for a popular product release, a restaurant opening, a free community event or a busy office. When the line gets close, the client takes your place or you complete the errand for them.

Good fit if: you're patient, don't mind early mornings, and can stay comfortable and polite for hours.

Your starter kit

  • Ozark Trail camping stool — ~$11 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

Dress for the weather in layers, bring a charged phone and power bank, and keep water and snacks with you.

What customers pay pros

Taskrabbit's errand cost guide puts waiting in line at $30 to $125, depending on how long the wait runs. (Taskrabbit)

As a beginner, quote below that range until you have reviews and photos of your work.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Watch your local news and social pages for big openings, launches and events, and post an offer to hold a spot a few days before.
  2. Offer friends a discounted first line-sit in exchange for a review and a referral.
  3. Charge a clear hourly rate with a minimum, so long waits are fair to you.
Watch out: some events, venues and government offices don't allow paid line holding or ticket resale, so check the rules first. Never hold a spot where you'd be blocking others unfairly, and don't wait alone in unsafe places or late at night.
06

Plant Watering

~$5 to start

You visit clients' homes while they're away and water their indoor plants and patio pots, following their instructions. Many clients also ask you to bring in the mail and check that everything looks fine.

Good fit if: you're trustworthy, reliable with schedules, and live close to the homes you'll visit.

Your starter kit

  • Expert Gardener 2 gallon watering can — ~$5 (Walmart · See options on Amazon)

Ask each client for written watering instructions for each plant, and send a quick photo after every visit.

What to charge

We couldn't find a reliable national cost guide for plant-watering visits, and prices vary a lot by area and by how many plants there are. Search local house-sitting and plant-sitting listings, note what they charge per visit, and price yours in that range. Put the visit schedule and price in writing.

Landing your first 3 customers

  1. Tell neighbors you know who travel for the holidays that you can water their plants while they're away.
  2. Post in your neighborhood group or Nextdoor with a simple price per visit and the area you cover.
  3. Ask happy clients to recommend you to friends who travel often.
Watch out: you'll be trusted with someone's keys and home. Get a written agreement with the visit dates, keep keys labeled with no address, never bring anyone else inside, and tell the client right away if anything looks wrong.

Before you take your first job

  1. Check local rules. Search "[your city] business license" to see whether you need one for an errand service. Rules vary by city and state.
  2. Check your car insurance. If you drive for these errands, ask your insurer whether your policy covers driving for paid work.
  3. Put it in writing. Send a short message with the errand, date, price and how you'll be paid back for any purchases before every job.
  4. Keep every receipt. Hand clients their receipts and tracking numbers, and keep copies for yourself.
  5. Track every dollar. Side income is taxable, so record what you earn, keep a log of business miles, and set some aside.
Pro tip

Sell prepaid 5-errand packs so busy clients book once and you get paid upfront.

FAQ

Which of these is cheapest to start?

Gift shopping is free to start with a free Google Voice number. Plant watering starts at about $5 for a 2 gallon watering can, and line sitting at about $11 for a camping stool.

Do I need a business license?

It depends on your city and state. Many places require a general business license for paid services, even part-time. Check your city's website or call the clerk's office before you start.

How much can I make?

We don't promise earnings. Where we show prices, they're what customers typically pay for the same errands, from published cost guides. As a beginner, expect to charge less until you have reviews.

Do I need a car?

Not for every idea. Line sitting and plant watering can work on foot or by transit if your clients live nearby. Package pickup and grocery runs are much easier with a car.

Can teens do these?

Plant watering for neighbors and line sitting with a parent's OK can work for teens. Anything involving driving or handling a client's money is better for adults. Check your state's rules for working minors.
